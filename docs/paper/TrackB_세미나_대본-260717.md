# Seminar Script — "Is There an Original?" (Track B)

## ACT I — The premise nobody could question  (slides 1–4, ~3 min)

**[SLIDE 1 — Title]**
[Let the title sit for three seconds before you speak.]
Every field that studies memory shares one silent assumption. Today I want to name it, break it, and replace it with something you can actually compute. This is a talk about a question we have never been able to ask — **and an artwork I built so that we finally can.**

**[SLIDE 2 — the tree]**
Here is the standard tool for how texts mutate across copies: a family tree. One original at the root, and every scribe's error branching down. It's powerful. But notice what it *forbids*. The instant you draw the tree, the root — the original — is already assumed. [gesture to the diagram] Even the most famous critique of the method, Bédier's, only asked *which* tree is correct. Nobody could ask whether a root exists at all. The tree makes that question ungrammatical.

**[SLIDE 3 — memory science]**
And it isn't just old manuscripts. Since Bartlett, in 1932, we've known that remembering is reconstruction, not playback. But listen to the word: *re-construction* presupposes something that was constructed first. Distortion. Contamination. Degradation. **The entire vocabulary of the field places the original at coordinate zero.** Every measurement we make is a distance from an origin we never questioned.

**[SLIDE 4 — the move]**
[Slow. This is the thesis.]
So here is the whole move of this paper, in one sentence. We stop assuming the original and measuring how far each version strays from it. Instead, we ask whether a single consistent original **could exist at all** — and we turn that yes-or-no into a computation. Existence stops being a premise. **It becomes a result.** [pause] To do that, I need one piece of mathematics. But first, an intuition — no equations yet.

---

## ACT II — The intuition  (slides 5–7, ~3 min)

**[SLIDE 5 — the grandmother]**
Three siblings remember their late grandmother. Their memories overlap in places, differ in others. Ask the question: if we collect all three, does one *true* grandmother come back? Common sense says yes — each saw a part, so stitch the parts together. The theory I'm defending says **no.** They will not stitch into one. There were only ever three different grandmothers. And the mathematics I'm about to show you is nothing but the machine that checks: can these pieces be stitched — yes or no?

**[SLIDE 6 — the seam]**
Now here's the subtle part — the part that makes this real mathematics and not a metaphor. The siblings don't just remember different *events*. Even where they remember the *same* moment, the connections differ. Both remember grandmother in the kitchen. For brother A it was warm — right after she forgave him. For brother B it was cold — right before a fight. **Same moment. Opposite of what it flows from.** You cannot fold these into one timeline. The conflict is not inside the scene. It lives in the *seam* between scenes. Hold onto that word — seam.

**[SLIDE 7 — cellular sheaf]**
The formal version. Take the scenes of a memory as points, and the admissible transitions between them as edges — that's the base space. Over it we build a cellular sheaf. A *section* is one playthrough: the emotional trajectory a person left across the scenes they visited. And *gluing* is exactly our grandmother question — do overlapping sections assemble into one global telling? [beat] One trap to flag now: a section has to carry the *seams*, the transitions — not just the values at each scene. If it only carried values, everything would trivially glue, and the whole theory would say nothing.

## ACT III — The derivation  (slides 8–10, ~3.5 min)

**[SLIDE 8 — cochain]**
[This is where the mixed audience needs you to slow down and point at each line.]
Here's the construction. Build a nerve graph: each run is a node, and two runs are joined by an edge if they share at least *m* scenes, weighted by how many. On each shared scene, take the difference between the two tellings — that's *d*. Its average is *g* — a single constant offset. Think of *g* as one run recalibrated onto the other. The residual *r* then asks: can these two runs be reconciled by that one shift alone? If the residual is at most *tau*, we call the edge **consistent** — these are precisely the pairs that "agree with their neighbor."

**[SLIDE 9 — rho]**
[Slow. This slide is the entire experiment.]
And here's the payoff. Let *h* assign every run its own best global recalibration — that's a single graph-Laplacian linear system, trivially small at this scale. Whatever that recalibration explains away is *reducible* — it's just per-run bias. **Rho is the fraction that survives it.** The cyclic, path-dependent residue that no per-run shift can remove. Rho greater than zero is exactly nonzero holonomy around loops. And rho equal to zero means one global telling fits every run — **"the original exists," computed, not assumed.** Rho is the whole experiment in one number.

**[SLIDE 10 — monodromy]**
Let me give you the picture behind that number. Monodromy — path dependence. [point at the loop] Walk the overlaps around a loop and come back, and the telling has *twisted*. A and B agree. B and C agree. C and A agree. And yet the three cannot be assembled into one. Like a Möbius strip: every local patch looks normal; only going all the way around reveals the twist. **This is why a flat pairwise metric is blind to it** — every neighbor is consistent, so the metric reports "all fine." Only the sheaf sees the global twist. Keep that — it becomes the test that could kill this theory.

## ACT IV — The instrument and the reframe  (slides 11–12, ~2 min)

**[SLIDE 11 — TEM as instrument]**
But there's a problem: this input didn't exist. Most applied sheaf theory runs on toy examples — three sensors, a handful of nodes. Hundreds of *real* emotional trajectories over a shared scene graph? Nobody had that dataset. So I built the instrument that produces it. The Etched Mutation is an interactive artwork. Each visitor moves through someone's memory, and their telling is recorded as a local section — and reshapes the terrain the next visitor inherits. **The artwork is the measurement device.** That is the unusual part: the instrument generates the very phenomenon it measures.

**[SLIDE 12 — the reframe]**
[Let this one breathe.]
And here's the moment the two halves meet. The artwork had, for months, been tracking something it called *contamination* — the mismatch between how two overlapping tellings connect. It turns out that quantity was the nameless version of disagreement energy. The obstruction to gluing doesn't *predict* contamination — **it is contamination, written in another language.** The art and the mathematics were describing the same thing without knowing it.

## ACT V — Rigor: falsifiability, method, results  (slides 13–17, ~4 min)

**[SLIDE 13 — falsifiability]**
A real theory has to be able to die. So here is the test that could kill this one. A reviewer will ask: wouldn't simple pairwise distances give you the same result? Is the sheaf just decoration? My defense requires one specific phenomenon to actually exist in the data: **tellings that agree with every neighbor, yet twist when you go around the loop.** If that signal is there, the sheaf earns its place — because nothing pairwise can reconstruct it. If it's absent, I lose, and this becomes a speculative essay, not a measurement. That is the falsification condition.

**[SLIDE 14 — pre-registration]**
One methodological point, made explicit. Every threshold, every null model, the entire go/no-go rule — all of it was written down and committed **before** I ran the experiment. This matters more than it used to. We now live in a moment where a fluent-sounding theory can be generated in minutes. So the thing that separates a theory that *lives* from one that merely *sounds good* is no longer eloquence — it's whether you staked a falsifiable claim before you saw the answer. Pre-registration here isn't a footnote. **It's the method.**

**[SLIDE 15 — pilot, ρ=0.18]**
Here's where the pilot stands. On thirty-nine runs, about eighteen percent of the disagreement is cyclic — the part no per-run recalibration removes. And in a thousand simulated worlds where an original genuinely exists, [point to the tail] not one reached that value; the p-value is below a thousandth. Now the honest framing: these thirty-nine runs are **not human beings.** They are language-model personas — simulations. But hold that thought, because it makes the finding stranger, not weaker.

**[SLIDE 16 — the table]**
[Point across the row as you read.]
Here's the full table — three choices of emotion-axis space against three null models. Read the decision row, A12. Rho is 0.18. Against N2 — the original-exists null — it beats every one of a thousand resamples, p below a thousandth; and N3, which keeps the axis correlations intact, agrees. Now look at N1: p equals one. **That is not a failure.** It says that shuffling the scene order produces *more* cyclic disagreement — so the real order carries structure that reduces it. And Exhibit A — a single loop twisted past the null's ninety-ninth percentile — shows up for the other axis sets but sits right on the boundary for the conservative decision axis. That borderline is exactly why the verdict is *weak-go*, not go. [beat] I'm showing you the number that argues against me, too.

**[SLIDE 17 — self-validation]**
One more slide, because a number is only as good as the instrument that made it. Before touching the real data, I ran the pipeline on two worlds where I already knew the answer. World one: a synthetic world built to have a single consistent original. The pipeline returns rho of three hundred-thousandths — essentially zero. Correct. World two: I planted one cyclic twist on a ring of runs. Rho jumps to point nine-nine-nine, **and Exhibit A points back at that exact ring.** So the instrument reads zero where the truth is zero, and localizes a twist where one exists. It was validated before it was believed.

## ACT VI — What it means, and where it goes  (slides 18–21, ~2.5 min)

**[SLIDE 18 — limits]**
Let me be plain about both sides. Why is the finding striking? These agents were *trained to be consistent* — coherence is their whole job. And even they couldn't be stitched into one original; they left an irreducible cyclic residue. If a machine built for consistency leaves that residue, a real human, remembering a real loss, should leave *more*. That becomes a pre-registered prediction for the live experiment. And the limits, stated plainly: no single dramatic loop yet for a clean figure; thirty-nine runs over ten scenes is modest statistical power; language-model personas carry their own bias. **The genuine test is the live audience — and that data is coming.**

**[SLIDE 19 — contribution]**
So what does the paper actually contribute? At the heart of variant theory is a claim — that variation is not degradation, but a mode of being. Beautiful, but until now, unfalsifiable. This work gives it a precise, testable meaning: **variant memory is a class of objects whose baseline state is disagreement energy greater than zero.** Not "the original is lost" as a lament — but "no consistent original exists" as a *measured property*, with a number attached.

**[SLIDE 20 — the medium]**
And let me close the loop on why this had to be an artwork, not just a study. The theory needs local sections that are *lived* — emotional trajectories a person actually walks through, moment to moment — not boxes ticked on a questionnaire. A static survey flattens exactly the seams the theory is about. Only an interactive work produces trajectories with real transitions in them. **So the medium isn't decoration bolted onto the research. It is the precondition of the measurement itself.**

**[SLIDE 21 — close]**
[Return to the opening. Slow.]
So, to close where we opened. Preservation believes the pieces stitch back into an original. Variant theory says the seams will not close — and for the first time, that refusal is not a poetic claim but a number you can compute, and a claim you could prove me wrong on. The pilot did it with simulated minds. What remains is to do it with **the seams of real people.**
[Two seconds of silence.]
Thank you.

## Delivery notes

- **Total ≈ 16–18 min.** If you need 12, cut Act III to one slide (merge 8+9) and drop slide 17.
- **The three places to slow down:** slide 4 (thesis), slide 9 (ρ), slide 16 (reading the table). Everything else can move.
